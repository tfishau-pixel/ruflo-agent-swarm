# FAR FROM HELP

### Trained-hands procedures for when the vet is 90 minutes away

*Supplement to **First Lambing Season** (NZ Edition). Written for a property whose nearest emergency vet is a ~1.5 hour drive away and may not always be reachable.*

---

## Why this document exists — and its rules

The main handbook marks several procedures **"trained hands only"** and says *ring the vet*. That is the right default when help is 20 minutes away. It is not a complete answer when help is 90 minutes away and may not answer the phone. In remote NZ farming, these procedures are **standard farmer skills**, taught by veterinary and agricultural extension services precisely because the farmer is the first responder. This supplement collects the published, authoritative versions of those techniques so that:

1. you can **learn them before lambing** (read now, practise what can be practised, get hands-on instruction where possible), and
2. in an emergency, you or a phone-guiding vet have the **exact steps in front of you**.

**The rules of engagement:**

- **Still ring first.** Distance changes what you do while help comes — it doesn't remove the call. Ring at the *first* sign of trouble (with 1.5 h travel, the old "watch for 30 minutes" becomes "ring at 10–15 minutes and watch while you talk"). A vet can talk you through everything below by phone or video call.
- **Get shown beforehand where you can.** Ask your clinic now — before lambing — for a pre-lambing session: stomach-tubing, the IP injection site, and an internal exam demonstration. One hour at the clinic converts every procedure below from "first time, alone, at 2 a.m." to "revision".
- **Know your Plan B: load and drive.** A ewe in trouble travels better than most people expect (bedded trailer or covered ute crate, lamb with her if born). Driving toward the vet — or meeting halfway — is often faster than waiting for them to be free. Decide this early, not at hour three.
- **Every procedure has stop conditions.** They are printed in each section. Stopping and driving is never a failure.

## 0. The remote playbook (read this once, before lambing)

| Do now, before lambing | Why |
|---|---|
| Program **both/all clinics within range** + after-hours numbers into your phone and print them | Reachability is your stated risk — have more than one number |
| Ask each clinic: after-hours protocol, whether they take video calls, expected travel time, whether they'll meet you partway | Turns a 90-min problem into a 45-min problem |
| Book a **pre-lambing training visit** (or attend a smallholder lambing workshop) | Tubing and IP injection are 10-minute skills to learn with a teacher |
| Set up a **video-call option** (FaceTime/WhatsApp) and test it in the lambing shed — check reception there | A vet who can *see* the presentation can guide you precisely |
| Prepare the **extended kit** (Section 5) | The extra items below are cheap; the drive isn't |
| Prepare **transport**: trailer or crate, bedded, tarp, torch | Plan B ready to execute |
| Write your property's RAPID number / directions where anyone can read them to a vet | Saves minutes when it matters |

**During any emergency below:** put your phone on speaker in your chest pocket, ring the vet, and work with them. If truly unreachable, these are the published steps.

## 1. Internal examination and correcting a malpresentation

*Sources: NADIS "Lambing Part 1 — the basics"; University of Wisconsin Extension "Visual Lambing Guide" (watch these videos before lambing — they show every scenario below on real ewes); Sheep 201/Purdue.*

### Before your hand goes anywhere near the ewe

1. **Ring the vet / start video call.** Describe or show what's presenting.
2. Wash your hands and arms in warm water with diluted disinfectant; fingernails short.
3. Long glove on; **cover it in obstetric lubricant — more than you think you need.** Re-lubricate every time you re-enter.
4. Restrain the ewe: for most corrections **cast her on her side**; for a leg-back, position her so the *missing leg's side is uppermost* (NADIS). For repelling a head it may be easier with her standing or hindquarters raised on a straw bale.
5. Work **between contractions; pause when she strains.** Fingertips together, hand shaped like a cone, enter slowly.

### First, identify what you feel — do not pull anything yet

Trace every limb to the body it belongs to. Front leg: fetlock and knee bend the **same** way. Hind leg: fetlock and hock bend **opposite** ways. A tail near hooves = hind end. Two lambs can tangle — confirm both legs you hold belong to the *same* lamb (follow each to the shoulder/hip).

### Leg back (one front leg back) — the most common fixable problem

1. Push the lamb's head/chest gently back toward the ewe (between strains) to make room.
2. Follow the missing shoulder down to the knee, then the cannon, then the fetlock.
3. **Cup the hoof in your palm** (this shields the uterine wall from the sharp toe — the single most important detail) and flex the knee.
4. Bring the hoof up and over the pelvic brim in one smooth motion, keeping it cupped.
5. With both legs and head now in the "diver" position, deliver with traction as below.
*A small lamb can sometimes be delivered with one leg back — a phone vet may advise it; don't decide that alone your first season.*

### Both front legs back ("hung lamb" — head out, no legs)

1. Keep the head moist and membranes off the nose throughout.
2. **Gently repel the head** back into the ewe between strains (easier with the ewe standing / rear raised). This feels wrong; it is the correct move and the lamb tolerates it.
3. Retrieve each front leg exactly as for a single leg back — cup every hoof.
4. If the head slips back once legs are up, a **lambing snare or soft rope behind the ears and through the mouth** (never around the jaw alone) holds it in the diving position while you deliver. This is precisely what the snare in your kit is for.
5. A head that is badly swollen may still deliver alive — but if you cannot repel it within a few minutes, **stop: load and drive.**

### Head back (two legs, no head)

The hardest common correction — the head must be found and brought round in a small space, and it flips back easily. **Attempt only with a vet on the phone/video guiding you.** The published sequence: repel the lamb, follow the neck to the head, cup the muzzle in your hand (never hook fingers in the eye sockets), bring it round into the canal, hold with a snare if it won't stay. Two failed attempts = load and drive.

### Hind legs first (soles up, hocks follow)

**Do not convert it — deliver it, promptly.** Once the hips enter the canal the cord pinches and the lamb will try to breathe inside the ewe. Confirm both hooves are hind feet of the same lamb, then pull both legs **steadily down toward the ewe's hocks with her strains, briskly through the ribcage stage** — this is the one delivery where measured speed matters (Wisconsin: "quick action is critical once the umbilical cord snaps"). Clear the airway the instant it's out; expect to do resuscitation.

### Breech (tail only, hind legs tucked)

Correction = repel the rump, find a hock, cup the hoof, flex it up over the brim; repeat for the second leg; then deliver as hind-first. It is done by farmers, but it needs room, patience, and a well-lubricated arm — **phone/video guidance strongly advised; two failed attempts = load and drive.**

### Two lambs at once

Repel the *second-furthest-forward* lamb; deliver the one already engaged (usually the one whose head is in the canal, or the hind-first one). The entire skill is confirming which legs belong to which lamb before pulling. **Phone guidance; if you can't sort the tangle in a few minutes, load and drive.**

### Traction — the universal rules (NADIS)

- Pull **only during her strains**, on **two legs + head in position** (or two hind legs).
- Direction is a **downward arc toward her hocks** — "down and around, never straight out".
- A normal assisted delivery takes **10–30 seconds of traction**; steady hands only, never ropes-to-fenceposts, never more force than your arms.
- **Stop conditions:** dry and swollen canal that lube doesn't fix · no movement with two well-applied strains · anything you can't identify · pain response escalating. Stop = load and drive (keep her penned and calm; keep exposed parts moist).

### After any assisted delivery

- Clear the lamb's airway immediately; rub hard; straw-tickle a nostril.
- Leave the cord intact 1–2 minutes if it hasn't broken (NADIS).
- **Check for another lamb**: with a clean re-lubed glove, or ballot (gently lift-and-feel) the belly in front of the udder.
- Iodine the navel; get colostrum in early — an assisted lamb is a slow lamb.
- Watch the ewe closely for 48 h (tears, infection, retained placenta); tell the vet what you did — an assisted ewe in NZ commonly warrants antibiotic cover, which is their call and their supply.

## 2. Stomach-tubing a lamb

*Sources: OMAFRA/Ontario "Care of the newborn lamb"; OSU Sheep Team; Cornell Cooperative Extension. Ten minutes with a vet or experienced farmer converts this from frightening to routine — ask at your pre-lambing visit. But the technique itself is published for farmers, and with a 90-minute vet it belongs in your hands.*

**Use it when:** the lamb *can hold its head up and swallow* but won't or can't suck (too weak, mouth too cold after warming to ≥37 °C, ewe unmilkable) and it needs colostrum now.
**Never tube:** a lamb that can't hold its head up or has no swallow · a lamb below 37 °C that is over ~5 h old (that lamb needs the IP injection first — Section 3) · never before an IP injection when one is indicated ("milk could kill the lamb" — SRUC).

1. **Measure first:** hold the tube alongside the lamb — nose to last rib. Mark that length (tape). That's how far it goes; it's further than you expect (most of the tube).
2. Sit; lamb upright on your lap, chest up, head in a natural nose-forward position (not stretched up).
3. Wet the tube tip with colostrum/water. Insert at the **side of the mouth**, over the tongue, slightly to the **lamb's left** (the food-pipe lies left of and behind the windpipe).
4. Advance slowly and gently. **Correct = the tube slides easily** and the lamb may chew and swallow it down; you can feel/see it pass down the left of the neck — with a finger on the throat you can feel **two tubes** (windpipe + your tube). **Wrong = resistance, coughing, struggling, or the tube stops short → withdraw completely and start again.** A tube in the windpipe kills; a tube in the stomach at the right length cannot.
5. Extra check: with the tube in, put your ear to the end — crying and airflow = windpipe, withdraw. Silence (or gurgles) = stomach.
6. Attach the 60 ml syringe **without the plunger** and let warm colostrum run in by gravity, or push very gently: **50 ml per kg, over about 5 minutes.** Never fast.
7. **Crimp/kink the tube firmly before withdrawing** it in one smooth motion (stops drips entering the airway on the way out).
8. Warm, dry pen; recheck in 30–60 min; repeat feeds to reach ~200 ml/kg by 24 h if it still won't suck.

## 3. Intraperitoneal (IP) glucose injection — the cold-lamb rescue

*Sources: Farm Advisory Service Scotland; SRUC; OSU Sheep Team; Cornell; the original research is Eales & Small, 1982 ("Resuscitation of hypothermic lambs", Vet Record). This is the procedure that saves the lamb the handbook says "do not warm first". Farmer-administered across the UK, Ireland and NZ; the margin for error is genuinely wide (the abdominal cavity absorbs glucose safely), which is why extension services teach it.*

**Use it when:** lamb **below 37 °C AND older than ~5 hours AND unable to swallow/hold its head up.** (Any lamb that can swallow gets warmed and tubed instead — Sections 2 and the handbook's decision tree.)

**Prepare (do the shopping before lambing):** 20% glucose/dextrose solution — from your vet or rural store. Or make it: **dissolve 20 g glucose powder in hot pre-boiled water, make up to 50 ml (40%), then dilute half-and-half with warm pre-boiled water** (SRUC) — result ≈20%, ~50 ml, at lamb-body temperature (warm, not hot, on your wrist). Dose: **10 ml per kg** — a typical 4–5 kg lamb gets that full ~45–50 ml. 50/60 ml syringe, new sterile **1-inch (25 mm) 19G-ish needle**.

1. Ring the vet (speaker pocket). Draw up the warmed solution; no air bubbles matters less here than speed — this lamb is dying of energy failure.
2. **Hold the lamb hanging vertically by its front legs** (a second person helps; alone, hold both forelegs in one hand, lamb's back against your legs). Gravity drops the gut organs away from the injection site.
3. Site: **2–3 cm (≈1 inch) below the navel and ~1 cm (≈½ inch) to the side of it.**
4. Insert the full needle **at 45°, angled toward the lamb's tail head** (rump). Through the skin and the wall — two faint pops.
5. **Inject slowly and steadily — the full dose.** No drawing back needed at this site.
6. Withdraw, rub the site, spray it with iodine.
7. **Now warm** (warm-air box, ~35–40 °C air, check every 20–30 min; recheck lamb temperature at 30 min). Expect a transformed lamb standing within 30–60 minutes.
8. Once warm (≥37 °C) and swallowing: **tube 50 ml/kg colostrum** (Section 2), then re-mother.

**Do not:** inject a lamb that can swallow (tube it instead) · use cold solution (worsens the hypothermia) · reuse needles between lambs · inject at the navel itself or forward of it.

## 4. Replacing a bearing (vaginal prolapse) and fitting a retainer

*Sources: Farmers Weekly step-by-step; Northland Veterinary Group (NZ); Agriculture Victoria "The prolapsing ewe"; OSU. NZ clinics publish this precisely because bearings happen on farms far from help. The vet-preferred version uses an epidural so she can't strain against you — which is why a phone call first is still step 1: they may say "bring her in" or talk you through it.*

**Check first — call/stop conditions (Farmers Weekly):** tissue that is **torn, black or cold** → do not replace; ring/drive (a badly damaged bearing can be unsalvageable — a vet must decide). Signs she is **actually lambing** (water bag, feet or tail palpable through the cervix behind the prolapse) → this is now a lambing problem, not just a bearing; ring immediately.

1. Two people if possible: one holds her steady, standing or gently backed against a rail; raising her hindquarters slightly (bale under back legs, or hold her rear up wheelbarrow-style) makes replacement far easier.
2. **Clean:** plenty of clean warm water with mild antiseptic (dilute iodine "weak-tea colour" — Northland Vets). Remove all dirt and bedding. Do not scrub.
3. **Lubricate** the whole surface generously.
4. **Replace:** first **lift the mass up toward her tail** (this un-kinks her bladder — often she'll pass urine, the ball shrinks, everything gets easier). Then with the **flat of a clean hand or a closed fist — never fingertips** — apply slow, steady, patient pressure. **Pause and hold when she strains; gain ground between strains.** It goes back gradually, then all at once. Ensure it's fully returned (your hand follows it in to full depth so the tip of the vagina is un-inverted).
5. **Retain:** fit the **bearing retainer ("spoon"/T-piece)** — tongue inside the vagina, wings tied firmly to the wool of her flanks with the tapes (Northland Vets) — or fit a **prolapse harness** per its instructions. Buy one of these *now*; improvising at midnight is miserable.
6. **Aftercare:** she needs pain relief and usually antibiotic cover — in NZ these are vet-supplied: ring the clinic, tell them what you did, and arrange it (they may leave supplies out for you given your distance — ask about this *before* lambing). Check the retainer twice daily for slipping and rubbing. **She can lamb past a spoon retainer** (it pushes out) but watch her closely at lambing; a harness must be released. Mark her: **bearings recur — do not breed her again.**

## 5. Extended kit — additions for the remote property

Add these to the handbook's kit list:

| Item | For | Source note |
|---|---|---|
| 20% glucose/dextrose (or glucose powder + measuring jug) | IP injection | Vet or rural store; ask vet to include in a pre-lambing pack |
| 50–60 ml syringes ×3, sterile 1" needles ×6 | IP injection | Rural store |
| Bearing retainer (spoon) **and** prolapse harness | Bearing | Rural store — buy before lambing |
| Lambing snare (head snare) | Holding a corrected head | Rural store; watch the Wisconsin video on its use |
| Second bottle of obstetric lube | Everything above | You cannot have too much |
| Iodine, extra | Navels + injection sites | — |
| Head-torch spare + power bank | 2 a.m. is when this happens | — |
| Printed copy of this supplement (laminated §§2–4) | The paddock has no wifi | — |
| Bedded trailer / ute crate ready | Plan B: load and drive | Test-load a calm sheep once |

**Ask your clinic for a "distance pack" at your pre-lambing visit.** Many rural NZ practices will, for a known client 1.5 h out, pre-supply: oral energy drench, injectable calcium (subcutaneous, with training), pain relief and antibiotic cover with instructions for defined situations. This is normal remote-client practice — but it must be arranged with *them*, in advance. That conversation is the single highest-value hour of your preparation.

## 6. Still genuinely vet-only — and the Plan B for each

| Problem | Why it stays vet-only | Your move when far from help |
|---|---|---|
| **Uterine prolapse** (huge mass, buttons, post-lambing) | Replacement needs an epidural + technique; forcing it can rupture an artery | Ring; keep her still/lying, mass in a clean wet towel, off the ground; **load and drive is usually right** — she travels lying, mass supported, if the vet says go |
| **Ringwomb** (cervix never opens) | Usually caesarean | Ring early (it declares itself over hours, so you have time to drive) |
| **Oversized lamb truly stuck** after correct position + failed gentle traction | May need caesarean | Load and drive — don't spend an hour trying |
| **Uncontrolled fresh bleeding** | Surgical | Ring; keep her still; drive if told |
| **Down ewe (milk fever / sleepy sickness)** | IV calcium is vet-only; **subcutaneous** calcium and oral energy drench are farmer tasks *with vet training/supply* | Arrange SC-calcium training + supply at the pre-lambing visit; then this becomes partly yours |
| **Caesarean, embryotomy** | Obvious | The drive is the treatment |

## 7. What the wider evidence says about your ewes (the research you asked for)

A specific search of the veterinary literature (including under the breed's German name, *Walliser Schwarznasenschaf*) confirms: **no peer-reviewed lambing/dystocia dataset exists for the Valais Blacknose.** The breed appears in Swiss population-genetics work (Signer-Hasler et al., 2019 — inbreeding and selection signatures across eight Swiss breeds) but not in obstetric studies. "Easy lambing" remains a breeder claim.

What the general sheep literature *does* establish, and how it applies to three well-fed VB ewes:

- **Dystocia tracks lamb birthweight** more than anything else: assistance rates are lowest around ~3.5 kg and climb steeply above ~4.5 kg; single lambs in well-fed ewes are the classic oversized risk (Smith 1977; Dorset, Merino and NZ Romney studies). *Practical: a VB ewe scanned with a single, on lush spring feed, is your most likely assisted birth — don't over-feed singles-bearing ewes in the last month, and watch her closest.*
- **Ewe size helps:** VB ewes are large-framed (70–90 kg — CABI/Wikipedia breed data), and pelvic size relative to lamb size is what matters (Richardson et al.). *Practical: mature VB ewes have room; a first-timer (two-tooth) lambing a big single is the higher-risk combination.*
- **Failure of the cervix to dilate is the most common maternal cause** of dystocia in sheep (302-case hospital series, 2022). *Practical: a ewe who paws and nests for 6–8 hours but never produces a water bag is exactly the "quiet emergency" the guides flag — with your travel time, ring at hour 4–6, not hour 8.*
- **Both sexes are horned**, but horn buds are soft and flat at birth — they play no role in dystocia. The fleece does: crutch, dag, and clear the udder before lambing.
