Card 1 · during labour

Labour has started

Green — observe
What you may see

Ewe apart from the flock, pawing/scraping the ground, up and down repeatedly, staring at her flank, lip-curling, soft bleating, a mucus string. No strong pushing yet.

Most likely

Stage 1 labour — the cervix is opening. Normal length 3–6 hours (first-timers sometimes longer).

Do now
  1. Note the time.
  2. Watch from a distance every 30–60 min.
  3. Get kit, towels, warm water and phone ready.
Do not

Don’t pen, chase or fuss her — interference stalls early labour.

Call the vet if

Intense restlessness for 6–8 h with straining never starting — or she turns dull or goes down instead of restless (possible sleepy sickness / milk fever → Card 12).

Next: water bag →

Card 2 · during labour

Water bag is visible

Green — clock running
Most likely

Stage 2 has begun. Lamb normally born within 30–60 minutes.

Do now
  1. Note the time — this is your reference point.
  2. Watch quietly; expect feet within ~30 min.
Do not

Don’t break the bag or pull membranes.

Call the vet if

1 hour after the bag with no lamb, or 30 min of hard straining with no feet appearing.

Card 3 · during labour

Feet are visible

Look closely, then decide
Three checks

Soles down or up? One foot or two? Nose or no nose?

You seeIt meansUrgency
Two hooves soles down + noseNormalGreen
One hoof + noseOne front leg backRed if no progress in 30 min
Two hooves advance, no nose followsHead turned backRed now
Two hooves soles up, no noseHind legs first — must be quickAmber → Red at 15–20 min
Soles up and a noseLamb upside downRed now
Three+ legs / mixed directionsTwo lambs togetherRed now
See the abnormal diagrams
Do now — normal case only
  1. Watch. Progress every 10–15 min = leave her to it.
  2. Gentle assist, only if presentation is confirmed normal (two soles-down hooves + nose) and she strains well but slowly: clean gloved hands, lots of lube, grip both legs above the fetlocks, pull gently only during her strains, downward in an arc toward her hocks.
  3. Any resistance, or no movement after one or two assisted strains → stop, ring the vet.
Do not

Never pull a single leg, a head, or anything you can’t identify. Never pull between contractions. No ropes, no force.

Call the vet if

Anything other than two soles-down hooves + a nose — or 30 min with no progress.

Card 4 · during labour

Head is visible

With feet — or without?
Most likely

Head + two feet = normal, watch it flow. Head without feet = legs back — a stuck birth. A head out and not advancing = stuck (often oversized single).

Do now — head only / stuck
  1. Ring the vet — say exactly what is showing.
  2. Keep the head moist: clean towel wetted with lukewarm water.
  3. Wipe membranes off the nose.
  4. Pen her; keep her calm and still.
Do not

Never pull on the head or jaw. Don’t push the head back yourself.

Call the vet if

Head without both feet: immediately. Vets deliver swollen-tongue lambs alive.

Card 5 · any time

Something red / pink is protruding

Identify it now
Tell them apart — size, surface, timing
Do now — bearing (before lambing)
  1. Ring the vet.
  2. Pen her quietly.
  3. Hold a clean towel soaked in clean lukewarm water over the tissue — keep it clean and moist.
Do now — uterine prolapse (after lambing)
  1. Ring the vet immediately — say “uterine prolapse”.
  2. Keep her still, ideally lying; keep the mass off the ground.
  3. Support and cover it with a clean lukewarm-wet towel.
  4. Keep her lamb(s) near so she stays calm.
Do not

Do not push either prolapse back yourself. No disinfectants on the tissue. Never let her run or be chased.

Call the vet if

Any tissue mass that stays out. Always.

Card 6 · during labour

Pushing — but nothing appearing

Red at 30 minutes
What you may see

Strong rhythmic straining — lying and pushing with head raised, up, circling, pushing again. Nothing at the vulva, or only a long-gone water bag.

Most likely

A lamb that can’t enter the canal: head or legs back, breech, two together, oversized — or a cervix that hasn’t opened (ringwomb). You don’t need to know which.

Do now
  1. Ring the vet — report the times.
  2. Pen her calmly on clean bedding.
  3. Set out warm water, soap, towels, lube and gloves for the vet.
Do not

Don’t go in to “have a feel” untrained. Don’t wait another half hour to be sure.

Breech warning

A breech ewe may strain only weakly and seem “not really in labour”. A ewe who paws and nests for hours but never produces a water bag belongs on this card too.

Card 7 · during delivery

Lamb is partly out

Red unless it completes now
What you may see

Head and chest out but hips stuck; a lamb hanging half-out; a hind-first lamb half-delivered. Once the chest is out the lamb tries to breathe — membranes on the nose now suffocate it.

Do now
  1. Clear the nose and mouth of membranes immediately — this one, you always do.
  2. Normal position + ewe straining: grip chest/shoulders (never the head) and ease down toward her hocks, with her strains.
  3. Hind-first half-out: steady downward pull on both hind legs with her strain — it must come now.
  4. No movement after two assisted strains → ring the vet, keep the airway clear.
Do not

No twisting hard, jerking, or bracing-and-hauling. Never pull straight backward — the curve toward the hocks matters.

Call the vet if

Two assisted strains produce no movement.

Card 8 · first minute

Lamb born — not breathing

Act in seconds
Do now
  1. CLEAR — membranes and fluid off nose and mouth, fingers and towel.
  2. TICKLE — clean straw deep in one nostril → triggers a gasp or sneeze.
  3. RUB — chest and ribs, hard, with a towel (mimics the ewe’s licking).
  4. DRAIN — brief supported head-down hold by the hind legs, a few seconds, while rubbing.
  5. Repeat tickle–rub cycles. A heartbeat with no breaths → keep going; many start at 1–2 minutes.
  6. Ring the vet at the 1-minute mark — keep working while you talk.
Do not

Don’t swing the lamb by its legs. Don’t give up early — wet lambs that look dead often aren’t.

Call the vet if

Not breathing after your first full cycle — or breathing but gurgly/rattly (fluid on the lungs).

Card 9 · newborn to day 3

Lamb is cold / weak / floppy

Thermometer first
Most likely

Hypothermia and/or starvation — one spiral: cold lambs don’t suck, unfed lambs get cold. Normal lamb temperature 39–40 °C.

Do now — by temperature
  1. Take its temperature. Dry the lamb, out of the weather.
  2. 37–39 °C → feed colostrum (must be able to swallow) + warm to ≥39 °C + back to mum, confirm sucking.
  3. <37 °C, under ~5 h old → warm first (warm-air box ~35–40 °C), feed once it can swallow.
  4. <37 °C, older than ~5 h → do NOT warm first — ring the vet. Its glucose is spent; warming without energy kills. It needs an abdominal glucose injection (vet / trained person) before warming.
  5. Can’t lift its head or swallow → nothing by mouth. Vet now.
Do not

Never feed or tube a lamb that can’t swallow. No hot-water immersion unattended, no direct contact with heaters, nothing hot touching the lamb.

Call the vet if

Below 37 °C and over ~5 h old · can’t swallow · not clearly improving within 30–60 min of warming.

Card 10 · first hours

Lamb will not suck

Amber at 2 h → Red at 4 h
Most likely

Wool over the udder (a Valais special), a wax-plugged teat, a sore udder, a rejecting ewe — or a weakening lamb. First feed belongs inside 2 hours.

Do now
  1. Trim/clear wool around the udder; strip each teat until milk squirts (clears the wax plug).
  2. Hold the ewe gently against a rail; guide the lamb’s mouth to the teat; a squirt of milk on its lips triggers sucking.
  3. Still no feed → milk the ewe into a clean container, bottle-feed 50 ml per kg (≈200 ml for a 4 kg lamb) — only if the lamb is warm and can swallow.
  4. No milk available → thawed frozen colostrum or ewe-specific colostrum powder, per label, body-warm. Never microwave or boil it.
  5. While you’re there: udder hot, hard, painful, or milk watery/bloody = mastitis → ring the vet.
Do not

Never stomach-tube unless you’ve been shown how. A cold floppy lamb goes to Card 9 before any feeding.

Call the vet if

Too weak to suck a bottle (needs tubing) · nothing achieved by hour 4 · udder abnormal.

Card 11 · during / after lambing

Ewe is bleeding

Drips and pools are Red
Normal vs not

Normal: blood-tinged fluids and light streaks; red-brown discharge (lochia) shrinking over 2–3 weeks, never foul.
Not normal: fresh red blood dripping, running or pooling; large clots; a pale, weak, panting ewe.

Do now
  1. Ring the vet — estimate amount and rate (“cupful”, “steady drip”, “pooling”).
  2. Keep her still, penned, calm — movement worsens bleeding.
  3. Leave the lamb with her.
Do not

Don’t insert or pack anything. No drugs.

Call the vet if

Any active fresh bleeding · foul-smelling discharge at any time (infection — also a vet call) · she is weak or pale.

Card 12 · before or after lambing

Ewe is down / weak / collapsed

Red — hours matter
What you may see

Before lambing: off feed, dull, seems blind, star-gazing or head-pressing, staggery, then down — sleepy sickness (pregnancy toxaemia). Or sudden trembling → flat, maybe bloated — milk fever (low calcium).
After lambing: down, dull, off feed ± fever or foul discharge (infection) or pale gums (bleeding).

Most likely

Sleepy sickness · milk fever · womb/udder infection · internal bleeding. You don’t need to decide which.

Do now
  1. Ring the vet — say: before/after lambing, eating?, discharge?, how long down.
  2. Prop her upright on her chest with a bale or rolled towel (a flat ewe bloats).
  3. Shelter from sun and rain; water within reach.
  4. Only if she can swallow and the vet advises it by phone: the oral energy drench from your kit.
Do not

Never drench a flat ewe or one that can’t swallow — it goes to her lungs. Don’t lift by the wool. Don’t wait until morning.

Call the vet

You’re on this card — ring. Milk fever and sleepy sickness respond dramatically to early veterinary treatment, and not at all to waiting.

Card 13 · after lambing

Placenta / afterbirth

Normal by 6 h · never pull
Most likely

Still hanging at 12–18+ h = retained placenta. The risk is womb infection (metritis).

Do now
  1. Note when she lambed; just watch until 12 h.
  2. You may trim membranes dangling below the hocks with clean scissors.
  3. Past 18 h → ring the vet for advice even if she seems well.
  4. Any dullness, fever or foul smell → ring now.
Do not

Never pull the placenta — it tears, and traction damages the uterus. No weights tied to it.

Call the vet if

Retained > 18 h · any smell · any sick ewe.

Still unsure?

Ring the vet and describe what you see. “A pink ball the size of an orange” or “one foot and a nose” is enough for triage. A photo sent to the clinic helps. Never feel wrong for calling — with three high-value ewes and a first season, your vet expects it.

Sources & scope

Companion to First Lambing Season — A Practical Guide for Valais Blacknose Ewes (NZ Edition). This is a recognition-and-triage tool for first-time owners, not a treatment manual: safe first aid only, early veterinary contact always. Compiled August 2026.

Timings and thresholds cross-checked against: Beef + Lamb New Zealand (lamb survival, colostrum); NADIS UK (lambing stages, prolapses); AHDB UK (colostrum 50 ml/kg by 2–6 h, 200 ml/kg per 24 h); Farm Advisory Service Scotland & SRUC (hypothermia bands and the 5-hour rule); Merck/MSD Veterinary Manual (retained membranes, prolapses); NZ veterinary clinic guidance (bearings, sleepy sickness, milk fever). Full linked bibliography: handbook, “Sources & further reading”.

Breed-specific claims for Valais Blacknose are breeder-reported unless stated. This guide does not replace veterinary advice for an individual animal.
