# FIRST LAMBING SEASON

### A Practical Guide for Valais Blacknose Ewes — New Zealand Edition

*For first-time sheep owners. Written for a small flock of four Valais Blacknose sheep, three ewes in lamb.*

*This guide helps you recognise what is normal, judge urgency, and act safely. It is not a substitute for veterinary care. When in doubt, ring your vet — early calls save lambs and ewes.*

---

## How urgency is shown throughout this guide

| Label | Meaning | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **🟢 GREEN — Normal** | Expected at this stage | Observe quietly from a distance |
| **🟠 AMBER — Caution** | Could go either way | Watch closely, note the time, get ready to act, consider ringing the vet for advice |
| **🔴 RED — Urgent** | Ewe or lamb at risk | **Ring the veterinarian now.** Do safe first aid only while you wait |

Never rely on colour alone — every label in this guide also carries the word (GREEN / AMBER / RED) so it works in grayscale and for colour-blind readers.

## 1. Quick-start overview

### The final two weeks — what normally happens

- The udder fills and firms ("bagging up"), starting 1–4 weeks before lambing.
- The vulva becomes pinker, softer and slightly swollen.
- The ligaments either side of the tail head slacken; the rump looks "sunken" in the last day or two.
- The belly drops lower; the ewe may look hollow in front of the hips.
- Appetite may dip slightly in the final days.
- The ewe may slow down, lie down more, and keep more to herself.

### The final 24 hours — what normally happens

- Ewe separates from the others and seeks a quiet spot.
- Restlessness: up and down, pawing/scraping the ground ("nesting"), sniffing the ground, looking at her flank.
- She may go off feed.
- A string of clear or slightly cloudy mucus from the vulva.
- Full, tight udder; teats may have a wax plug.

### The three stages of labour (know these cold)

| Stage | What happens | Normal duration | When it becomes AMBER/RED |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** — cervix opens | Restless, paws, up/down, separates, may show a mucus string. No visible straining yet, or only mild | **3–6 hours** (first-timers can take longer, up to ~12 h of intermittent behaviour) | AMBER if intense restlessness with no progression to straining after 6–8 h; RED if she becomes dull, down and unresponsive |
| **Stage 2** — lamb delivered | Water bag appears, then feet and nose; strong abdominal straining; lamb born | Lamb usually born within **30–60 min of the water bag**; within **~30 min of feet showing** | **RED: 30 minutes of hard straining with no progress**, or 1 hour after water bag with no lamb |
| **Stage 3** — placenta passed | Ewe licks lamb, placenta (afterbirth) hangs and drops | Usually **2–3 hours**, up to 6 h is acceptable | AMBER→vet call if not passed by **12–18 hours**, or sooner if she is sick. **Never pull it** |

Timing varies between ewes — treat these as ranges, not stopwatches. What matters most is **progress**: each stage should visibly move forward.

### The warning signs that always matter

- 🔴 Hard straining for 30 minutes with nothing appearing — or no progress after something appeared.
- 🔴 A head showing with no feet, only one foot, a tail only, or hooves soles-up.
- 🔴 Any large red/pink mass of tissue protruding from the ewe (prolapse) — before or after lambing.
- 🔴 Fresh, dripping or pooling blood (more than light streaks).
- 🔴 Ewe down, unable to rise, dull, grinding teeth, or "star-gazing" — before or after lambing.
- 🔴 Lamb not breathing, or cold, floppy and unable to lift its head.
- 🟠 Lamb not sucking within 2 hours of birth.

### When to call the vet — one-line rule

> **If you have watched for 30 minutes and things are not clearly moving forward — or you see any RED sign — ring the vet. A "too early" call costs a phone call. A late one can cost the ewe and the lamb.**

Have the vet's daytime **and after-hours** numbers saved in your phone and written up in the shed before lambing starts (see the Emergency Contacts panel at the end).

## 2. Preparing before lambing

### Environment checklist

- [ ] **Lambing paddock**: sheltered, well-drained, close to the house, good pasture cover, checked for hazards (troughs lambs can drown in, gaps, dogs).
- [ ] **Shelter**: natural shelter belts, hedges or a shed; in NZ spring the killer is **wind + rain + cold together**, so shelter from the prevailing southerly matters more than temperature alone.
- [ ] **Lambing pen(s)**: at least one clean pen (about 1.5 × 1.5 m or larger) under cover, ready in case a ewe or lamb needs confining. For three ewes, two pens is comfortable.
- [ ] **Bedding**: deep, dry straw or untreated wood shavings; replace between ewes.
- [ ] **Lighting**: working light in the shed and a charged head torch for paddock checks at night.
- [ ] **Clean water**: fresh water in the pen at a height lambs cannot drown in; a lambing ewe drinks a lot.
- [ ] **Hygiene**: pens cleaned and disinfected before the season; remove birth fluids/bedding between ewes; wash hands or glove between animals.
- [ ] **Isolation area**: somewhere to separate a sick ewe or a mismothered lamb.
- [ ] **Containment**: gates/hurdles you can move calmly; Valais are quiet and people-friendly, which makes penning easy — use that, don't chase.
- [ ] **Mud management**: rotate gateways/feed areas; a muddy lambing site drives navel infection and chilling.

### Lambing kit

Assemble this in one clean, lidded bin at least two weeks before the first due date.

| Item | Why it is needed | When it is used | Beginner-safe? | Important caution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Disposable gloves | Hygiene for you and the ewe | Any handling of ewe, lamb or fluids | ✅ Yes | Change between animals |
| Long obstetric gloves | Protect ewe from infection if an internal check is ever needed | Only if a trained person examines the ewe | ⚠️ With instruction | An internal exam is not a beginner task — see Section 6 |
| Obstetric lubricant (plenty) | Reduces trauma in any assisted delivery | Any assistance or exam | ⚠️ With instruction | Use generously; never assist "dry" |
| Clean towels (many) | Drying lambs, grip, warmth | Every birth | ✅ Yes | Wash and dry between uses |
| Iodine navel spray or dip (as sold by NZ rural suppliers) | Prevents navel ill/joint ill | Within 15 min of every birth; repeat once 2–4 h later | ✅ Yes | Cover the whole cord and the skin where it joins the belly |
| Digital thermometer | Objective check of lamb (and ewe) temperature | Any dull, cold or weak animal | ✅ Yes | Lamb normal 38.5–39.5 °C; below 39 °C in a newborn needs action (Section 9) |
| Head torch + spare batteries | Night checks, hands free | Every night round | ✅ Yes | — |

**Beginner-safe vs needs-instruction:** anything marked ⚠️ or ❌ should be demonstrated to you by your vet or an experienced sheep farmer **before** lambing. Many NZ vet clinics run pre-lambing (“lamb rearing” / smallholder) evenings in winter — ask yours.

## 3. Understanding late pregnancy

### What you will see, and what it means

**Udder development ("bagging up").** The udder enlarges and firms from about 1–4 weeks out. In Valais, long belly wool can hide it — part the wool or feel gently. In the last days the udder is tight and the teats fill and point slightly outward.

**Vulva.** Becomes longer, softer, pinker and slightly swollen in the final 1–2 weeks.

**Pelvic ligaments.** The ligaments between tail head and pin bones soften; the area either side of the tail looks sunken in the last 24–48 h.

**Behaviour.** Slower, lies down more, may separate from the flock, may go off feed in the last day. Pawing the ground, circling, sniffing the ground, and getting up and down repeatedly signal that stage 1 labour is starting. Valais are unusually people-oriented — a normally friendly ewe that stands off on her own is telling you something.

**Mucus.** A clear or slightly cloudy mucus string in the final hours is normal.

### Normal versus concerning — late pregnancy

| Sign | Normal (🟢 GREEN) | Watch (🟠 AMBER) | Call the vet (🔴 RED) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Udder | Gradual filling over 1–4 weeks | Sudden hot, hard, painful udder; one side much larger | Hot painful udder + sick ewe (possible mastitis) |
| Vulva | Soft, pink, mild swelling; mucus string near lambing | Persistent thick discharge weeks early; small pink bulge when lying that goes back when standing | Any tissue mass that stays out (bearing/vaginal prolapse — Section 6); smelly or bloody discharge before labour |
| Appetite | Slight dip in final days | Off feed a full day in late pregnancy | Off feed + dull/"star-gazing"/apparently blind/staggery — possible **sleepy sickness** (pregnancy toxaemia), an emergency |
| Behaviour | Quieter, separates, nests | Restless for hours without progressing | Down and can't rise, tremors, teeth grinding — sleepy sickness or **milk fever** (low calcium), an emergency |
| Movement | Slower, heavier | Limping (check feet — Valais can be footrot-prone in wet NZ conditions) | Unable to stand |

**Two late-pregnancy emergencies to burn into memory** (both look like "the ewe is down or dopey *before* lambing"):

- **Sleepy sickness (pregnancy toxaemia / twin-lamb disease):** ewe off feed, dull, seems blind, head-pressing or star-gazing, staggery, eventually down. Caused by energy shortfall in ewes carrying multiples. 🔴 RED — ring the vet; survival depends on early treatment.
- **Milk fever (hypocalcaemia):** sudden onset, often after a stress (yarding, weather, transport): wobbly, trembling, then flat on her side, sometimes bloated. 🔴 RED — this is fatal in hours untreated, and very treatable by a vet. Do not try to drench a flat-out ewe (she can inhale it).

## 4. Normal lambing — step by step

### Stage 1 — the cervix opens (3–6 hours, sometimes longer in first-timers)

*What she does:* separates, paws/nests, up and down, stares at flank, lip-curls, may bleat softly. Mild, irregular straining late in this stage.
*What you see:* mucus string; udder tight; vulva slack.
*Your job:* **watch from a distance.** Note the start time. Do not pen, chase, or fuss her — interrupting stage 1 can stall labour. Check again every 30–60 minutes.
*Concerning:* frantic behaviour that goes on 6–8+ h without any straining; or she becomes dull rather than restless.

### Stage 2 — delivery (usually under an hour)

*Sequence you should see:*

> **Water bag appears → bag bursts or hangs → two front hooves → nose between/just above the hooves → head out → chest out (the slow bit) → rest of lamb slides free → ewe turns and licks lamb**

*Timings:*
- Water bag → lamb: usually **30–60 minutes**.
- Feet visible → lamb: usually **within 30 minutes**.
- Straining is strong and rhythmic; visible progress every 10–15 minutes once feet show.

*Your job:* stay back and time it. Approach only when the lamb is out, and only if needed.

*Concerning — the 30-minute rule:* 🔴 **hard straining for 30 minutes with no progress** (nothing appearing, or the same two hooves not advancing) means the lamb may be stuck. Ring the vet. See Section 6.

### Stage 3 — placenta (afterbirth)

*What you see:* red-purple membranes hanging within an hour or two; passed usually by **2–3 hours** (up to 6 h fine). The ewe may eat it — normal.
*Concerning:* not passed by 12–18 hours, or she is dull/off feed/smelly discharge → ring the vet. **Never pull hanging membranes** — they can tear and the traction can damage the uterus.

### The whole picture

| ~Time | Event |
|---|---|
| −4 h to −½ h | Restless, nesting, mucus (Stage 1) |
| 0 min | Water bag appears |
| 10–40 min | Feet, then nose |
| 30–60 min | **Lamb born** |
| +5 min | Lamb shaking head, sitting up on chest |
| +15–30 min | Lamb attempts to stand |
| +30–60 min | Lamb finds teat, **first suck** |
| +10–45 min | Next lamb, if twins/triplets (same sequence) |
| +2–3 h | Placenta passed (one per lamb, or joined) |

## 5. Normal and abnormal lamb presentation

**Presentation** = which part of the lamb is coming first, and how its legs and head are arranged. This decides whether a birth is straightforward or stuck.

### Normal — "diving" position

Two front hooves, **soles pointing down**, one usually slightly ahead of the other, with the **nose resting between or just above them**. The lamb comes out like a diver: feet, nose, head, shoulders, body.

*(See the labelled diagrams in the handbook HTML/PDF and the Emergency Guide. In the field: two toes + a nose = 🟢 GREEN; anything else = at least 🟠 AMBER.)*

### Also deliverable, though watch it closely: hind legs first

Two hooves with **soles pointing up** and no nose; the hocks (backward-pointing "knees") follow. Lambs can be born hind-first, but the umbilical cord pinches during delivery — once it starts it must finish quickly. 🟠 AMBER: ring the vet if delivery isn't progressing within 15–20 minutes; experienced help delivers these promptly.

### Abnormal presentations — recognition table

| What you see at the vulva | Likely arrangement inside | Urgency | Should a beginner try to correct it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two hooves soles-down + nose | **Normal** | 🟢 GREEN — observe; assist only as in Section 6 | Assistance sometimes, correction not needed |
| One hoof + nose, second hoof missing | **One front leg back** | 🟠→🔴 within 30 min if no progress | ❌ No — needs the head pushed back and the leg brought up: trained hands |
| Nose/head only, swelling tongue, no hooves | **Both front legs back** | 🔴 RED now | ❌ No — head-only deliveries jam at the shoulders; head may need repelling: vet |
| Two front hooves, no nose after they advance | **Head turned back** | 🔴 RED now | ❌ No — one of the harder corrections; vet |
| Tail only, or a soft dome, no hooves | **Breech** (bottom-first, hind legs tucked forward) | 🔴 RED now — the ewe may not even strain hard, so don't be fooled by a "quiet" ewe making no progress | ❌ No — hind legs must be fetched in a small space: vet |
| Two hooves **soles-up**, no nose | **Hind legs first** | 🟠 AMBER — deliverable, must be quick | Only with experienced help present or on vet phone guidance |
| Hooves upside down and nothing advances; or lamb's spine feels/looks toward the floor | **Lamb upside down** | 🔴 RED | ❌ No |
| Three or more legs, or two heads' worth of parts, mixed soles up/down | **Two lambs coming together** | 🔴 RED | ❌ No — sorting out which leg belongs to which lamb is expert work |

**The honest rule for a first season:** you are not expected to correct a malpresentation. Your job is to **recognise "not normal" early and get the right hands there fast** — vet, or an experienced sheep-farming neighbour while the vet travels. That judgement, made at 30 minutes rather than 3 hours, is what saves lambs.

## 6. Common lambing complications

*Format for every condition: what it is → what you may see → urgency → what you can safely do → what not to do → call the vet if…*

### Prolonged labour / failure to progress

**What it is:** any stage that stalls. **What you may see:** straining with nothing appearing; or feet/nose appeared and stopped advancing; or restlessness that never becomes labour. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED at 30 minutes of hard straining with no progress (or 1 h after the water bag with no lamb). **Safe to do:** confine her calmly if easy; time everything; ring the vet and describe exactly what you can see; have warm water, soap, towels, lube ready. **Do not:** repeatedly disturb her; insert a hand "to check" without training; give any drug. **Call the vet if:** the 30-minute rule trips — don't wait to be sure.

### Dystocia — difficult or obstructed birth

**What it is:** the umbrella term for a lamb that cannot be delivered without help (malpresentation, oversized lamb, ringwomb, two together). **What you may see:** as above, plus an exhausted ewe who stops trying. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do — if a lamb is in the normal diving position (two soles-down feet + nose) and visibly moving but slow:** with clean gloved hands and lots of lubricant you may grip the two legs (above the fetlocks) and pull **gently, only during her strains, downward in a curve toward her hocks**. If one pull-with-strain produces no movement, stop and ring the vet. Never more force than one person's steady arms. **Do not:** pull on a head, a jaw, or a single leg; pull between contractions; pull anything you cannot identify; use ropes/mechanical force. **Call the vet if:** any resistance, any confusion about what you're feeling, or 5 minutes of gentle assistance achieves nothing.

### Oversized lamb

**What it is:** lamb too big for the pelvis — classic with single lambs in well-fed ewes. **What you may see:** normal presentation but stuck at the head/shoulders; big swollen tongue on a protruding head. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do:** keep the exposed head moist (clean wet towel), keep membranes off the nose, ring the vet. **Do not:** apply traction to the head. **Call the vet:** immediately — these can need a caesarean, and the sooner the call, the better both survive.

### Ringwomb — the cervix fails to open

**What it is:** incomplete cervical dilation; the door doesn't open. **What you may see:** hours of stage-1 behaviour, maybe a water bag or membrane visible, but no feet and weak straining. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED once she's been at it 6–8 h without progress (sooner if unsure). **Safe to do:** nothing internal; ring the vet — true ringwomb usually means caesarean. **Do not:** attempt to stretch anything. **Call the vet if:** persistent stage-1 with no stage-2.

### Uterine inertia — the uterus stops pushing

**What it is:** contractions weaken/stop (exhaustion, low calcium). **What you may see:** labour that fades away; ewe dull. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do:** ring the vet (this often accompanies milk fever). **Do not:** wait "in case she starts again".

### Vaginal prolapse — "bearing" (BEFORE lambing)

**What it is:** the vagina pushes out through the vulva under late-pregnancy pressure; NZ farmers call it a **bearing**. **What you may see:** a smooth pink/red rounded mass, orange-to-grapefruit sized, at the vulva — at first only when she lies down, later staying out. **Urgency:** 🟠 AMBER if small and appearing only when lying; 🔴 RED once it stays out, is drying, dirty, dark, or she's straining on it. **Safe to do while waiting:** pen her quietly; keep the tissue clean and moist (clean, wet, lukewarm towel held over it); keep other stock and flies off. If your vet has previously shown you replacement + retainer technique you may follow *their* instructions. **Do not:** push it back with unwashed hands or without your vet's guidance; put a dry or dirty prolapse back; leave her unwatched — a bearing can hide/complicate labour. **Call the vet if:** any prolapse that stays out. Also: a bearing ewe needs a plan for her lambing — tell the vet she's due, and don't breed her again (bearings recur).

### Uterine prolapse (AFTER lambing)

**What it is:** the whole uterus turns inside-out and comes out — usually within hours of lambing. **What you may see:** a **large** (rugby-ball scale) red/purple mass hanging to the hocks, covered in raised button-like bumps (**cotyledons** — the attachment sites; this is how you know it's uterus, not a bearing). **Urgency:** 🔴 RED — life-threatening emergency. **Safe to do while the vet travels:** keep her still and quiet (ideally lying); support and cover the mass with a **clean towel soaked in clean lukewarm water**; keep it off the ground; keep lambs with her if she's calm. **Do not:** attempt to push it back (it will be replaced under an epidural by the vet); let her run; wash with anything stronger than clean water/saline. **Call the vet:** immediately, and say the words "uterine prolapse" — clinics triage this as drop-everything.

### Retained placenta

**What it is:** afterbirth not passed. **What you may see:** membranes still hanging 12–18+ hours after birth, or a smell developing. **Urgency:** 🟠 AMBER at 12–18 h if she's bright; 🔴 RED if she's dull, off feed, feverish or the discharge stinks (metritis — womb infection). **Safe to do:** you may trim (cut, with clean scissors) dangling membranes below the hocks so she doesn't tread on them; keep her somewhere clean; watch appetite and demeanour twice daily. **Do not:** pull, ever; tie weights to it. **Call the vet if:** >18 h, or any sickness.

### Excessive bleeding

**What it is:** haemorrhage from the birth canal or uterus. **What you may see:** normal is *light* blood-tinged fluid and streaks; abnormal is **dripping, running or pooling fresh blood**, or large clots, or a pale weak ewe. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do:** keep her still and quiet; do not obstruct or pack anything; note how much and how fast for the vet. **Do not:** go internal "to find it". **Call the vet:** immediately.

### Sleepy sickness — pregnancy toxaemia (see also Section 3)

**Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do while waiting:** if she can swallow and your vet advises it on the phone, an oral energy drench sold for this purpose (e.g. propylene-glycol-based products from rural suppliers) may be given; offer good feed and water; shelter her. **Do not:** drench a ewe that is flat or can't swallow; assume she'll "come right" — untreated ewes die, usually with their lambs. **Call the vet:** at the first "off feed + dopey" late-pregnancy ewe.

### Milk fever — hypocalcaemia

**Urgency:** 🔴 RED — hours matter. **Safe to do:** shade/shelter, prop her upright on her chest (sternal) with a bale so she doesn't bloat. **Do not:** drench her; inject calcium yourself without training (under-skin calcium exists but dosing/placement need instruction; into-the-vein calcium is vet-only). **Call the vet:** immediately — response to treatment is dramatic and diagnostic.

### Mastitis

**What it is:** udder infection. **What you may see:** one side hot, hard, swollen, painful; ewe lame-ish behind, won't let lambs suck; milk watery, clotted or bloody; in the worst form the udder skin turns cold and blue-black. **Urgency:** 🟠 AMBER caught early; 🔴 RED if she's sick or the skin is discoloured. **Safe to do:** check the udder (gently strip a squirt from each teat onto a dark surface) daily in week one; keep lambs fed by bottle if she won't feed them. **Do not:** ignore a lamb that suddenly seems hungry — a starving lamb is often the first sign of mastitis. **Call the vet if:** heat + pain + abnormal milk, or any sick ewe. Antibiotics/anti-inflammatories are veterinary decisions in NZ (restricted veterinary medicines).

### Ewe unable to stand / collapse / suspected infection

**What you may see:** down, can't rise; or fever signs — dull, fast breathing, off feed, hot ears, temperature over 40 °C; or cold, pale and flat. Causes range from milk fever and sleepy sickness to metritis, mastitis or internal bleeding — you don't need to diagnose it. **Urgency:** 🔴 RED. **Safe to do:** sternal propping, shelter, water within reach, note her temperature if you can take it safely. **Do not:** lift by the wool; drench a flat ewe. **Call the vet:** immediately.

### Terminology — four things that come out of a ewe, told apart

| Structure | What it is | What it looks like | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water bag (fetal membranes)** | Fluid-filled sac ahead of the lamb | Translucent whitish/amber **bag or hanging membrane**, fluid inside, often bursts | 🟢 Normal — start your stage-2 clock |
| **Vaginal prolapse ("bearing")** | Vagina pushed out — usually BEFORE lambing | **Smooth pink/red ball**, orange→grapefruit size, no bumps | 🔴 Vet (AMBER only if tiny and intermittent) |
| **Uterine prolapse** | Whole uterus out — AFTER lambing | **Large ragged red/purple mass with button-like bumps** (cotyledons), can hang to hocks | 🔴 Vet — emergency |
| **Placenta (afterbirth)** | The lamb's life-support, passed after birth | Red-purple **membranes with buttons**, hanging then dropping free, often eaten | 🟢 Normal if within ~6 h; never pull |

## 7. Immediate newborn lamb care

### First 5 minutes

1. **Is it breathing?** Watch the chest. If membranes cover the nose/mouth, **clear them at once** with your fingers and a wipe of towel.
2. **Not breathing?** Wipe nostrils → tickle a nostril deep with a clean piece of straw (triggers a sneeze/gasp) → rub the chest and back briskly with a towel. You may hold the lamb briefly **head-down by the back legs** to let fluid drain while rubbing — a few seconds, supported, not swinging. If no breathing within about a minute of this, ring the vet while continuing to rub and tickle.
3. **Let the ewe take over.** Put the lamb at her nose. Her licking dries, warms and bonds. Only towel-dry yourself if she won't, or the weather is foul.
4. **Navel:** soak/spray the entire cord and its base with iodine.
5. **Step back.** Watch from a distance. Bonding in the first hour sets up everything.

### First 30 minutes — 🟢 normal looks like

Lamb shakes its head, sits up on its chest within minutes, makes attempts to stand by 15–30 minutes, bleats; ewe licks constantly, makes a low rumbling nicker.

### First 2 hours — the one thing that matters most

**The lamb must suck.** Normal: finds the udder, wiggles its tail hard while sucking, then sleeps curled and full. Confirm it — do not assume. If it has not fed by 2 hours, act (Section 8).
Helpful and safe: quietly trim any long belly/udder wool obscuring the teats (a real Valais issue), and check both teats give milk — strip each until the wax plug clears and milk squirts.

### First 24 hours

- Repeat navel iodine once at 2–4 h. The cord should dry and shrivel; a wet, red, swollen or smelly navel = 🟠 ring the vet.
- Weigh the lamb (record it). Healthy Valais lambs — like most breeds — should then gain roughly 250 g+/day; weight is your lie-detector.
- Watch for **meconium** (first, tar-black poo) and urination.
- Full lamb = curled, sleeping, stretches on rising, tight little belly. Hungry lamb = hunched back, tucked-up empty triangle behind the ribs, bleating, following the ewe and butting constantly, or standing with head low. Hungry now = starving by tonight — act same-day.

## 8. Colostrum — the single biggest lever you have

**Why:** lambs are born with almost no immune protection and small energy reserves. Colostrum (the ewe's thick yellow first milk) provides antibodies, concentrated energy and a laxative — but the gut can only absorb the antibodies for a short window, best in the **first 6 hours**, largely closed by 24 hours.

**How much, how soon (well-supported guidance):**

| Time | Target | 4 kg lamb example |
|---|---|---|
| Within 2 h of birth (6 h at the outside) | **50 ml per kg bodyweight** | 200 ml |
| First 24 h total | **~200 ml per kg**, split over 4+ feeds | 800 ml |

*(NZ industry guidance commonly says "at least 10% of bodyweight in the first 6–24 h"; UK/AHDB guidance says 200 ml/kg — 20% — over 24 h. The safe practical read: 10% is the floor, closer to 20% is the target, front-loaded early. A lamb sucking a well-uddered ewe manages this on its own.)*

**Has it fed? Evidence, not hope:** you saw sucking with tail-wiggling; belly feels full; lamb sleeps then stretches; ewe's teats look drawn. If none of these by 2 h → milk the ewe and bottle-feed, or use backup.

**Sources, best first:** (1) its own mother, milked into a clean container if the lamb won't suck; (2) frozen colostrum from another of your ewes; (3) **ewe-specific commercial colostrum powder** (NZ rural suppliers) mixed exactly per label. Cow colostrum is an inferior emergency fallback — ask your vet before relying on it (volume and rare anaemia issues).

**Thawing/warming:** stand frozen colostrum in warm (not hot) water; body-temperature when fed. **Never microwave or boil** — heat destroys the antibodies.

**Bottle vs tube:** a lamb that is warm and can suck → bottle. A lamb too weak to suck → it needs **stomach-tube feeding, which you must not attempt untrained** (a tube in the windpipe drowns the lamb). Get the vet or an experienced farmer — and use the moment to be taught for next time.

## 9. Hypothermia and weak lambs

Cold, wet and windy NZ spring weather kills more lambs than anything else. Starvation and chilling are one spiral: cold lambs don't suck; unfed lambs get cold.

**Take the temperature — don't guess.**

| Temperature | Status | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 39–40 °C | 🟢 Normal newborn | Carry on |
| 37–39 °C | 🟠 **Mild hypothermia** | Act now: dry, shelter, feed, warm |
| Below 37 °C | 🔴 **Severe hypothermia** | Follow the age rule below exactly |

**The decision tree (memorise the 5-hour rule):**

1. **Dry the lamb, get it out of the weather.**
2. **Temperature 37–39 °C?** → Feed colostrum (suck or bottle if it can swallow), warm it, return to mum once ≥39 °C and vigorous. 
3. **Below 37 °C and lamb under ~5 hours old?** → It still has birth energy reserves: **warm first**, then feed as soon as it can swallow.
4. **Below 37 °C and older than ~5 hours?** → Its energy is gone. **Warming before energy can kill it** (warming burns its last glucose). It needs a **glucose injection into the abdomen (intraperitoneal) before warming — a vet/trained-person procedure. Ring the vet now.** Warm only after energy is on board.
5. **Any severely cold lamb that can't hold its head up or swallow** → 🔴 vet, now. Never feed by mouth or tube — it will inhale it.

**Safe warming:** warm-air box at ~35–40 °C (covered box, towels, heat lamp *above* not touching, or wrapped warm — not hot — water bottles/wheat bags), checking every 20–30 minutes; or against your skin inside your jacket while you organise. **Not safe:** hot-water immersion unattended, direct contact with heaters, hair dryers on hot held close, or "warm and hope" for a flat lamb over 5 hours old.

**Back to mum** once ≥39 °C, vigorous and fed — re-mother in a pen and confirm sucking before you relax.

## 10. Ewe care after lambing

**🟢 Normal:** on her feet licking the lamb within minutes; eats and drinks keenly (offer lukewarm water — many ewes drink litres); rumbling nicker to the lamb; lets it suck; placenta by ~6 h; discharge (called **lochia**) red-brown, becoming scant over 2–3 weeks, **never foul-smelling**.

**Check daily for the first week:**

- [ ] Eating, drinking, chewing cud
- [ ] Udder: soft after feeding, both sides even, both teats giving milk (strip each on day 1)
- [ ] Discharge: reducing, no stink
- [ ] Placenta passed (find it, or watch for it — she may eat it)
- [ ] Moving freely, no straining
- [ ] Mothering: keeps the lamb close, answers its bleat

**🟠/🔴 Not normal:** off feed or dull (temperature over 40 °C = 🔴); foul-smelling or pus-like discharge (metritis) = 🔴; fresh bleeding = 🔴 (Section 6); continued straining after all lambs born (check — another lamb? prolapse coming?) = 🔴; hot painful udder (mastitis) = 🟠→🔴; rejecting the lamb = 🟠 pen them together in a small space, restrain her gently for supervised sucks, ring the vet/an experienced farmer if it isn't resolving within hours; down/unable to rise = 🔴.

**Move or leave?** Leave ewe + lamb(s) on the birth site for at least a few hours if weather allows — moving too early is a top cause of mismothering. Move calmly to shelter (carry lamb low, in her sight, walking slowly backward in front of her) if weather turns, and pen ewe + lambs together 24–48 h for bonding, especially with twins or a first-time mum.

## 11. Twins and multiple births

- **Another lamb coming?** After a lamb, the ewe rests then usually gets restless and strains again. Typical interval: **10–45 minutes**. She may also just deliver the next while licking the first.
- **How to tell she's done:** she settles into steady licking/feeding and stops straining; her flanks look slack rather than tight. (Your scanning result — record it on the ewe card — tells you how many to expect. Trust the scan.)
- **🔴 Concerning:** renewed hard straining >30 min with no lamb; or the scan said two and only one has arrived after an hour of calm; or she looks unwell.
- **Every lamb gets colostrum.** With twins, confirm each one sucking — the strong twin will happily take both shares. Weigh both; the lighter one gets priority checks.
- **Mismothering:** ewes can walk off with lamb one while lamb two arrives, or favour one twin. Pen the family together 24–48 h; mark ewe and lambs with matching numbers before any move.
- Triplets in a Valais would be unusual but not impossible — three mouths generally need supervised feeding or a bottle top-up; ask your vet.

## 12. First 72 hours — monitoring checklists

**Lamb — twice daily minimum:**

- [ ] Sucking observed (tail-wiggle), belly full
- [ ] Warm mouth, bright eye, stretches on rising
- [ ] Posture: straight back — not hunched/tucked-up
- [ ] Breathing quiet — no rattling, grunting or fast panting
- [ ] Peeing and pooing (meconium passed day 1; then yellow milk poo; watch it doesn't glue the tail down — "dags off" gently)
- [ ] Navel dry and shrivelling — not wet, hot, swollen or smelly
- [ ] Weight: gaining (~250 g+/day)
- [ ] No swollen joints, no reluctance to stand (navel/joint ill = 🔴 vet)
- [ ] Not "watery mouth" — drooly, cold-mouthed, bloated, tucked-up lamb in days 1–3 = 🔴 vet

**Ewe — daily:**

- [ ] Appetite and water intake strong
- [ ] Udder even, soft after sucking, no heat/pain
- [ ] Temperature (only if she seems off): 38.3–39.9 °C normal
- [ ] Discharge reducing, never smelly
- [ ] Placenta accounted for
- [ ] Sound on her feet
- [ ] Mothering solidly (answers lamb, stands to let it feed)

## 13. Valais Blacknose — breed-specific notes

**Reasonably established (breed societies / NZ breed community):**

- The NZ flock descends from UK embryos, first lambs born here 2017; the NZ Valais Blacknose Sheep Society (under the NZ Sheepbreeders Association) maintains the breed standard. Numbers are small and animals valuable — which is itself a management fact: **set a low threshold for calling the vet.** The economics that make commercial farmers wait don't apply to a $5,000 ewe, and shouldn't.
- **Fleece grows extremely fast (~30 cm/year; shorn twice yearly).** Practical consequences: **crutch/dag the back end and trim around the udder before lambing** (visibility of the vulva, hygiene, and teat access for the lamb); watch for wool over the eyes ("wool blindness"); a sodden full fleece is heavy and chilling in NZ spring storms — shelter matters even more than for other breeds.
- **Both sexes are horned.** Lamb horn buds are no obstacle at birth. Handle ewes by shoulders/chest, never by horns near lambing.
- An alpine breed on soft, wet NZ ground: breeders commonly report **feet need proactive care** (footrot/scald risk) — a lame ewe does a poor job of lambing and mothering, so check feet pre-lambing. *(Breeder observation, consistent with general NZ experience of imported wool breeds.)*

**Breeder-reported (treat as anecdote, not established veterinary evidence):**

- Valais are widely described by breeders as **relatively easy lambers with moderate birth weights and strong maternal instincts**, and can cycle outside the usual season. Reports of head/shoulder-related difficulty exist in some lines. There is little peer-reviewed lambing data for the breed — so run this season on the standard timings and thresholds in this handbook, not on breed reputation.
- Their **tameness is real and useful**: a Valais ewe will often let you check her udder, lamb and discharge without a yarding drama. Use quiet daily handling before lambing so checks are routine for her.

**Record what you learn:** your three ewes' records (back of this handbook) become your own breed-specific evidence for next season.

## 14. New Zealand seasonal and practical notes

- **The weather is the predator.** NZ lamb losses spike in southerly storms — cold + wet + wind. Watch the forecast against due dates; bring ewes into the sheltered paddock/shed *before* the front arrives, not during.
- **Outdoors vs pens:** healthy ewes lamb well outdoors on a sheltered, clean paddock; use pens for bonding, bad weather, assisted births, or any concern. Clean pens between families — warm wet bedding grows the bugs that cause navel ill and watery mouth.
- **Vet access:** rural NZ after-hours help can be 30–60+ minutes away. That travel time is *why* the 30-minute call rule exists — ring at the point of doubt so the vet is driving while things are still saveable. Ask your clinic now: after-hours number, expected travel time, what they want you to have ready, and whether they run pre-lambing training.
- **Products:** iodine navel sprays, obstetric lube, lamb feeders/tubes, colostrum powders, lamb covers and thermometers are all stocked by NZ rural suppliers (Farmlands, PGG Wrightson, Shoof, vet clinics) — buy before August. Antibiotics, calcium injections, pain relief and most treatments are **restricted veterinary medicines in NZ**: they come via your vet, which is another reason to be registered with a clinic before lambing.
- **Your legal baseline:** the Code of Welfare for Sheep and Beef Cattle (MPI) requires that ewes get timely assistance with birthing difficulty — "watching and hoping" past the thresholds in this book isn't just risky, it falls short of the code.
- **Biosecurity/hygiene basics:** gloves for everything involving birth fluids (campylobacter, toxoplasma and other zoonoses live there — **pregnant women should avoid contact with lambing ewes entirely**); wash up between animals; compost/bury placentas where dogs can't get them.

| What you see | What it likely means | Do | Urgency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straining 30 min, nothing appearing | Lamb stuck or not entering canal | Ring vet now; keep her penned and calm | 🔴 RED |
| Water bag an hour ago, no lamb | Stalled stage 2 | Ring vet now | 🔴 RED |
| One foot + nose only | Front leg back | Ring vet; do not pull | 🔴 RED |
| Head out, no feet | Both legs back — will jam | Ring vet; keep head moist; do not pull head | 🔴 RED |
| Feet out, no nose | Head turned back | Ring vet; do not pull | 🔴 RED |
| Tail only / dome, no feet | Breech | Ring vet — even if she seems calm | 🔴 RED |
| Two hooves soles-up | Hind legs first — deliverable but must be fast | Ring vet; assist only with experienced help/phone guidance | 🟠 AMBER→🔴 |
| Hard straining between lambs >30 min | Next lamb stuck | Ring vet | 🔴 RED |
| Lamb born, not breathing | Airway blocked / stunned | Clear nose NOW, straw-tickle nostril, rub hard, brief head-down drain; ring vet if >1 min | 🔴 RED |
| Lamb cold/weak/floppy | Hypothermia ± starvation | Temp it. 37–39 °C: dry, feed, warm. <37 °C: **if >5 h old do NOT warm first — vet for glucose** | 🔴 RED |
| Lamb won't suck by 2 h | Starvation clock running | Milk ewe + bottle if it can swallow; can't swallow → vet (tube = trained only) | 🟠 AMBER→🔴 |
| Smooth pink ball at vulva (pre-lambing) | Bearing (vaginal prolapse) | Keep clean/moist; pen her; ring vet | 🔴 RED |
| Huge red mass with buttons (post-lambing) | Uterine prolapse | Keep her still; cover with clean wet towel; ring vet NOW | 🔴 RED |
| Fresh blood dripping/pooling | Haemorrhage | Keep her still; ring vet now | 🔴 RED |
| Membranes hanging >18 h | Retained placenta | Ring vet; never pull | 🟠 AMBER (🔴 if sick) |
| Ewe down / dopey / "blind" (before or after) | Sleepy sickness / milk fever / other | Prop upright on chest, shelter; ring vet NOW; don't drench if flat | 🔴 RED |
