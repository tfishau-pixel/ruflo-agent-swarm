# LAMBING EMERGENCY GUIDE

### What am I seeing — and what do I do next?

*Companion to **First Lambing Season** (handbook). This is the rapid-reference version: one problem per card, minimal reading, urgency first. The interactive version is `emergency-guide.html` — save it to your phone before lambing starts.*

**Urgency labels:** 🟢 GREEN — observe · 🟠 AMBER — monitor/prepare · 🔴 RED — ring the veterinarian now.

> **The one rule: 30 minutes of hard straining — or of your own trying — without progress means RING THE VET.**

## 1. Labour has started

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Ewe apart from the others, pawing/scraping the ground, up and down repeatedly, staring at her flank, lip-curling, soft bleating, mucus string from the vulva. No strong pushing yet.

**MOST LIKELY** — Stage 1 labour: the cervix is opening. Normal length 3–6 hours (first-timers sometimes longer).

**URGENCY** — 🟢 GREEN.

**DO NOW**
1. Note the time.
2. Watch from a distance every 30–60 min.
3. Get kit, towels, warm water and phone ready.

**DO NOT** — Pen, chase or fuss her; interference stalls early labour.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Intense restlessness >6–8 h with no straining ever starting, or she turns dull/down instead of restless.

## 2. Water bag is visible

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — A translucent, whitish/amber fluid-filled bag at the vulva, or hanging membranes if it has burst. (Smooth solid pink ball instead? → Card 5.)

**MOST LIKELY** — Stage 2 has begun. Lamb normally born within 30–60 minutes.

**URGENCY** — 🟢 GREEN, clock running.

**DO NOW**
1. Note the time — this is your reference point.
2. Watch quietly; expect feet within ~30 min.

**DO NOT** — Break the bag or pull membranes.

**CALL THE VET IF** — 🔴 One hour after the bag with no lamb, or 30 min of hard straining with no feet appearing.

---

## 3. Feet are visible

**Look at the feet. Soles DOWN or soles UP? One foot or two? Nose or no nose?**

| You see | It means | Urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Two hooves, soles **down**, + nose | Normal diving position | 🟢 GREEN — lamb within ~30 min |
| Two hooves soles down, **no nose** appearing as they advance | Head may be turned back | 🔴 RED |
| **One** hoof + nose | One front leg back | 🔴 RED if not delivered within 30 min |
| Two hooves, soles **up**, no nose | Hind legs first — deliverable but the cord pinches; must be quick | 🟠 AMBER → 🔴 if not progressing in 15–20 min |
| Three+ hooves / mixed directions | Two lambs coming together | 🔴 RED |

**DO NOW (normal case)** — Watch. Progress every 10–15 min. If she is progressing, do nothing.

**Gentle assist — ONLY if presentation is confirmed normal (two soles-down feet + nose), she is straining well but slow:** clean gloved hands, lots of lube, grip both legs above the fetlocks, pull gently **only during her strains**, downward in an arc toward her hocks. Any resistance, or no movement after one or two assisted strains → stop, ring the vet.

**DO NOT** — Pull a single leg, a head, or anything you can't identify. Never pull between contractions. Never use ropes or force.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Anything other than two soles-down feet + nose; or 30 min with no progress.

## 4. Head is visible

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — A nose or whole head out, possibly with a swollen tongue. Check: are there **two front feet** with it?

**MOST LIKELY** — Head + two feet = normal, watch. **Head with no feet = both front legs back — the shoulders will jam.** A swollen-tongue head that isn't advancing = stuck lamb (often oversized).

**URGENCY** — 🟢 if head + both feet and progressing. 🔴 RED if head only, or no progress.

**DO NOW (head only / stuck)**
1. Ring the vet — say exactly what is out.
2. Keep the head moist: drape a clean towel wetted with clean lukewarm water.
3. Wipe membranes off the nose so it can breathe if the chest starts moving.
4. Keep the ewe penned and as calm as possible.

**DO NOT** — Pull on the head or jaw, ever. Do not push the head back yourself.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Head without both feet: immediately. A swollen tongue does not mean the lamb is dead — vets deliver these alive.

---

## 5. Something red/pink is protruding

**Four possibilities — tell them apart by size, surface and timing:**

| Looks like | Timing | It is | Urgency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Translucent bag/membranes, fluid | During labour | Water bag — normal | 🟢 (Card 2) |
| **Smooth** pink/red ball, orange→grapefruit size | **Before** lambing | **Bearing** (vaginal prolapse) | 🔴 RED |
| **Large** red/purple mass **with button-like bumps**, may hang to hocks | **After** lambing | **Uterine prolapse** | 🔴 RED — top emergency |
| Red-purple membranes with buttons, hanging loose | Hours after lambing | Placenta — normal | 🟢 (Card 13 if still there at 12 h+) |

**DO NOW — bearing (before lambing)**
1. Ring the vet.
2. Pen her quietly.
3. Keep the tissue clean and moist: hold a clean towel soaked in clean lukewarm water over it.

**DO NOW — uterine prolapse (after lambing)**
1. Ring the vet immediately — say "uterine prolapse".
2. Keep her still, ideally lying; keep the mass off the ground.
3. Support and cover it with a clean towel soaked in clean lukewarm water.
4. Keep lamb(s) nearby so she stays calm.

**DO NOT** — Push either prolapse back in yourself. Do not wash with disinfectants. Do not let her run or be chased.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Any tissue mass that stays out. Always.

## 6. Ewe is pushing but nothing is appearing

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Strong rhythmic straining, lying and pushing with head raised, getting up, circling, pushing again. Nothing at the vulva — or only a water bag that appeared ages ago.

**MOST LIKELY** — A lamb that can't enter the canal: head or legs back, breech, two together, oversized — or a cervix that hasn't opened (ringwomb).

**URGENCY** — 🔴 RED at **30 minutes** of hard straining with nothing. (Also RED: 1 h after water bag, no lamb.)

**DO NOW**
1. Ring the vet — report the times.
2. Pen her calmly; clean bedding.
3. Set out warm water, soap, towels, lube, gloves for the vet.

**DO NOT** — Go in to "have a feel" untrained. Do not wait another half hour to be sure.

**CALL THE VET IF** — You are on this card, call. **Breech warning:** a breech ewe may strain only weakly and seem "not in real labour" — treat a ewe who paws and nests for hours but never produces a water bag as this same emergency.

## 7. Lamb is partly out

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Head and chest out but hips stuck; or lamb hanging half-out; hind-first lamb half-out.

**MOST LIKELY** — Hip-lock or a tight fit. Once chest is out the lamb tries to breathe — membranes on the nose now suffocate it.

**URGENCY** — 🔴 RED unless it completes within a couple of strains.

**DO NOW**
1. **Clear the nose and mouth of membranes immediately** — this the beginner always does.
2. If the lamb is in the normal position and the ewe is straining: grip the chest/shoulders (never the head), and ease **down toward her hocks with her strains**.
3. A hind-first lamb half-out must come now: steady downward pull on both hind legs with her strain.
4. No movement with gentle traction → ring the vet while keeping the airway clear.

**DO NOT** — Twist hard, jerk, brace your feet and haul, or pull straight backward (the curve matters).

**CALL THE VET IF** — Two assisted strains produce no movement.

---

## 8. Lamb born but not breathing

**URGENCY** — 🔴 Act within seconds; ring vet at the 1-minute mark while continuing.

**DO NOW**
1. **Clear** membranes and fluid off nose and mouth with fingers/towel.
2. **Tickle** deep inside a nostril with a clean piece of straw — triggers a gasp/sneeze.
3. **Rub** the chest and ribs hard with a towel (mimics the ewe's licking).
4. **Drain**: hold the lamb briefly head-down by the hind legs, supported against your leg, a few seconds while rubbing.
5. Repeat tickle–rub cycles. Heartbeat but no breaths → keep going; many start after 1–2 minutes.
6. Ring the vet if not breathing by ~1 minute — keep working while you talk.

**DO NOT** — Swing the lamb by its legs (whirling risks injury and drives fluid the wrong way); give up early — wet lambs that look dead often aren't.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Not breathing after your first cycle; or breathing but gurgly/rattly (fluid on the lungs).

## 9. Lamb is cold / weak / floppy

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Lamb lying flat or hunched, slow or absent suck, cold mouth, weak bleat, head droop. Often after a wet cold night, or a lamb that never fed.

**MOST LIKELY** — Hypothermia and/or starvation. **Take its temperature — don't guess.** Normal 39–40 °C.

**URGENCY** — 37–39 °C: 🟠 AMBER, act now. Below 37 °C: 🔴 RED.

**DO NOW**
1. Thermometer first. Then dry the lamb and get it out of the weather.
2. **37–39 °C** → feed colostrum/milk (it must be able to swallow), warm to ≥39 °C, return to mum, confirm sucking.
3. **Below 37 °C, lamb under ~5 h old** → warm first (warm-air box ~35–40 °C), feed as soon as it can swallow.
4. **Below 37 °C, older than ~5 h** → 🔴 **do NOT warm it first.** Its glucose is gone; warming without energy kills. It needs an abdominal glucose injection — vet/trained person. Ring now.
5. Can't lift its head / can't swallow → nothing by mouth; vet now.

**DO NOT** — Feed or tube any lamb that can't swallow. No hot-water immersion, direct heater contact, or unattended heat sources.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Below 37 °C and over ~5 h old; can't swallow; or not clearly improving within 30–60 min of warming.

## 10. Lamb will not suck

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Lamb standing but not finding the teat; butting the wrong places; ewe circling away; or lamb simply weak. Clock matters: first feed should be inside **2 hours**.

**MOST LIKELY** — Wool over the udder (a Valais special), a blocked wax plug in the teat, a sore udder, a rejecting ewe, or a weakening lamb.

**URGENCY** — 🟠 AMBER at 2 h unfed → 🔴 RED if weak, cold, or 4+ h unfed.

**DO NOW**
1. Trim/clear wool around the udder; strip each teat until milk squirts (clears the wax plug).
2. Hold the ewe gently against a rail; guide the lamb's mouth onto the teat. A squirt of milk on its lips triggers sucking.
3. Still not feeding → milk the ewe into a clean container and **bottle-feed 50 ml per kg** (≈200 ml for a 4 kg lamb) if the lamb is warm and can swallow.
4. No milk available → thawed frozen colostrum or ewe-specific commercial colostrum powder, mixed per label, body-warm.
5. Check the udder while you're there: hot, hard, painful, or watery/bloody milk = mastitis → vet (Card 12's ewe checks apply).

**DO NOT** — Stomach-tube unless you have been shown how. Never microwave/boil colostrum. Don't force a cold floppy lamb to drink (→ Card 9 first: warm bands and 5-hour rule).

**CALL THE VET IF** — Lamb too weak to suck a bottle (needs tubing); nothing achieved by hour 4; or the udder is abnormal.

## 11. Ewe is bleeding

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Normal: blood-tinged fluids and light streaks during and after lambing; red-brown lochia (discharge) for up to 2–3 weeks. Abnormal: **fresh red blood dripping, running or pooling**, large clots, a pale/weak/panting ewe.

**MOST LIKELY** — Abnormal bleeding = a tear or uterine haemorrhage.

**URGENCY** — 🔴 RED for any active fresh bleeding.

**DO NOW**
1. Ring the vet — estimate the amount ("cupful", "steady drip", "pooling") and how long.
2. Keep her still, penned, calm — movement worsens bleeding.
3. Leave the lamb with her.

**DO NOT** — Insert anything or pack the vagina; give any drug.

**CALL THE VET IF** — Any dripping/running/pooling blood; foul-smelling discharge any time (infection, not bleeding, but also a vet call); or she is weak/pale.

## 12. Ewe is down / weak / collapsed

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Before lambing: off feed, dull, "blind"-seeming, star-gazing, staggering, down (**sleepy sickness**); or sudden trembling→flat, maybe bloated (**milk fever**). After lambing: down, dull, off feed, possibly fever or foul discharge (infection), or pale (bleeding).

**MOST LIKELY** — Sleepy sickness (pregnancy toxaemia), milk fever (hypocalcaemia), metritis/mastitis, or haemorrhage. You do not need to decide which.

**URGENCY** — 🔴 RED. Hours matter for all of these.

**DO NOW**
1. Ring the vet — describe: before/after lambing, eating?, discharge?, how long down.
2. Prop her upright on her chest (sternal) with a bale/rolled towel — a flat ewe bloats.
3. Shelter from sun/rain; water within reach.
4. If she can swallow and the vet advises it by phone, give the oral energy drench from your kit.

**DO NOT** — Drench a flat ewe or one that can't swallow (it goes to the lungs). Don't lift by the wool. Don't wait overnight to "see how she is in the morning".

**CALL THE VET IF** — You're on this card. Ring.

## 13. Placenta / afterbirth concern

**WHAT YOU MAY SEE** — Red-purple membranes with button-like bumps hanging after lambing, normally passed by 2–3 h (up to 6 h fine). The ewe may eat it — normal. One placenta per lamb (sometimes joined).

**MOST LIKELY** — Still hanging at 12–18+ h = **retained placenta**. Risk is womb infection (metritis).

**URGENCY** — 🟢 under 6 h. 🟠 AMBER 12–18 h and she's bright. 🔴 RED if she's dull, off feed, feverish, or it smells foul.

**DO NOW**
1. Note when she lambed; just watch until 12 h.
2. You may **trim** (clean scissors) membranes dangling below the hocks so she doesn't tread on them.
3. Past 18 h: ring the vet for advice even if she seems well.
4. Any sickness or stink: ring now.

**DO NOT** — **Never pull the placenta** — it tears and the traction damages the uterus. No weights tied to it.

**CALL THE VET IF** — >18 h retained; any smell; any sick ewe.

## 14. I am not sure what I am looking at

Answer three questions:

1. **Is anything protruding from the ewe?**
   - Translucent bag/fluid → Card 2. Feet → Card 3. Head → Card 4. Smooth pink ball or large bumpy mass or membranes → Card 5/13.
2. **Is she straining?**
   - Hard, >30 min, nothing to show → Card 6.
3. **Is there a lamb on the ground?**
   - Not breathing → Card 8. Cold/floppy → Card 9. Won't suck → Card 10. Ewe down or bleeding → Cards 11–12.

Still unsure → **ring the vet and describe what you see.** Describing "a pink ball the size of an orange" or "one foot and a nose" is enough for a vet to triage you. Photos help — take one, send it if your clinic uses messaging.

**Never feel wrong for calling.** With three high-value ewes and a first season, your vet expects the calls.
